A Man for All Markets

Edward O. Thorp

Overview

In A Man for All Markets, Edward O. Thorp revisits his life. Growing up through the war years, Thorp came from a simple household with both parents working graveyard shifts. He and his sibling would be left alone for most of the day, which let him experiment early on with chemistry. Thorp adopted a mindset of self-experimentation from an early age — testing whether what he was told or had read was actually true. This mentality would later drive his systems to beat the casinos as well as his success in the investment world.

He originally started university as a chemistry student, switched to physics after disagreements with a professor, and ultimately switched to mathematics when he realised that a physics PhD would take ten years and that all the maths he would have to learn along the way would qualify him for a maths PhD anyway. After reading a research journal claiming a system could beat the game of 21, Thorp followed his experimentation approach, walked into the casinos, and tested it. He found there was a real edge and developed his point count system, which is outlined in Beat the Dealer.

Having proven that casinos could be beaten, Thorp began working with Claude Shannon on a wearable computer that could narrow down where a roulette ball would land to within ten pockets — enough to generate large gains, and enough for the casinos to sabotage their car. From there, spurred by early investment losses, Thorp set out to play the largest casino in the world: Wall Street. Through his firm Princeton Newport Partners, Thorp would front-run the quantitative revolution, being among the first to apply Brownian motion and independently deriving the Black-Scholes formula for options pricing before Black and Scholes published it. Most of his plays were market neutral — buying and shorting the warrant against the underlying asset, betting on the spread converging. Towards the end of the book Thorp also explains the 2008 crash through the lens of CMOs, CDOs, and CDSs.

Key Takeaways

Thorp followed a market neutral strategy throughout his career, and these pair trades are still executed by trading firms today. Beyond automated relative-value trades, he was also hunting large arbitrage opportunities where the market acted irrationally, such as the COMS and PALM episode. His explanations of the major market crashes — 1929, the dot-com bubble, and 2008 — are brilliantly simple. His story is truly remarkable from youth through to everything he pioneered.

Who Should Read This

It is always good to learn from the best, and reading biographies like this is one of the best ways to do that. Thorp also discusses non-transitive rules and raises the question of whether old systems could beat newer systems. For that reason alone, reading this is worthwhile — to see how it started and whether old ideas could be reused in new markets.

Rating

5 out of 5, this has been my favourite book I have read so far.
